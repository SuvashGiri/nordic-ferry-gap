OPPORTUNITY · NORDIC FERRIES

Norway electrified its ferries. The rest of the Nordics are next.

The customers are big and buying. The ferry designers are small. The opportunity is to build a Nordic ferry-design group.

Ferries are roads across water. In Norway, more than half of the national-road ferries now run on batteries or hydrogen. Next door, most still burn diesel.

The customers: big ones are buying, smaller ones are stuck

Ship designers do not own ferries. They sell drawings and engineering hours to the operators and agencies that do. So the question for a designer is simple: are the customers ordering?

Demand comes from a few large, well-funded customers. A hypothesis worth testing: large customers may prefer larger suppliers.

The suppliers: a few giants, many small

Norwegian ship design has two tiers. At the top sit a few groups that design and build, or design at industrial scale:

VardNorwegian, owned by Italy's Fincantieri. 8,000+ employees, seven yards in Norway, Romania, Vietnam and Brazil, its own design arm.
UlsteinThird-generation family-owned, Ulsteinvik. NOK 3.0 billion revenue in 2025. Yard plus a design arm known for the X-BOW hull.
Kongsberg MaritimeNorwegian listed industrial group. More than 800 vessels built to its UT designs.

Sweden and Finland are not empty markets. FKAB did the production design for Trafikverket's new electric ferries, while Finland's Deltamarin is part of China Merchants Group. A Norwegian platform needs a reason to win across borders, not just more engineers.

Below them is a handful of independent design firms with 10–50 people, and a long tail of one-man offices. No public source gives market shares, but the gap in scale is clear.

Consolidation has started. NSK Ship Design bought Multi Maritime, designer of 45 ferries and 29 all-electric designs, in 2026 (shaded above). RINA bought Finland's Foreship in 2025 for retrofits and new fuels.

But merging designers is not enough.

Multi Maritime lost money five years in a row selling engineering hours. Put five offices like that together and you get one bigger office that still sells hours. Scale may help a firm qualify for fleet programmes. It does not, by itself, improve margins.

The large groups point to two models worth testing for an independent ferry-design group:

1 · Sell the same design many times
Industrial precedent

Ulstein sold 88 designs from 1999 to 2012, 63 of them with its patented X-BOW hull, and let other yards build them.

For ferries

Many short Nordic crossings are alike, so a few standard electric classes could cover them. Draw once, sell in four countries.

2 · Stay with the ship after delivery
Industrial precedent

Vard ties design to systems, upgrades and service for the vessel's whole life.

For ferries

Norway's electric fleet started in 2015 and is ageing. But battery swaps and system upgrades often go to integrators such as Corvus or Siemens, or to the yard. A designer only keeps this work if it sells the conversion engineering itself.

The real test: who keeps the savings?

If a group reuses a design, the next ferry needs fewer engineering hours. The question is whether the designer keeps that saving as margin, or the customer simply gets a lower bill.

THE OPPORTUNITY

A Nordic ferry-design group

Combine two or three independent designers into one group that can serve the big customers in all four countries, and is built to stop selling only hours.

  1. Scale to match the buyers. Torghatten, Molslinjen, Finferries and Trafikverket are large and funded. A group can bid on whole fleet programmes, not single ships.
  2. Sell the same design many times. A few standard electric classes for all four countries.
  3. Stay with the ship. Conversions and upgrades as the first electric fleet ages.
  4. Keep the people. Ownership and lock-ins for the 15–40 key engineers from day one. This is the single biggest risk after a deal.
  5. Win beyond Norway. Local presence or partners in Sweden and Finland, where the next orders are.

Why now: major operators have consolidated and are prioritising fleet renewal; Sweden is earlier in its transition, and consolidation among designers has begun.

What could break it

  • The people leave. A design firm is 15–40 key engineers. After a deal, the best ones can walk. This is the biggest single risk.
  • Reuse is lower than hoped. If only 15–20% of an electric ferry design carries over to the next route, the economics above barely move. Real numbers from past projects are needed.
  • Public buyers squeeze price. In tenders, savings tend to pass to the buyer. Pricing power is more realistic with private operators.
  • Lifetime work goes elsewhere. Integrators and yards may take the upgrades.
  • Ferries are alike, not identical. Length, capacity, quay, charging power, ice and tide differ. If only a small part of a design can be reused, "draw once, sell four times" falls. It has to be tested against real routes at Trafikverket, Finferries and NFI.
  • Norway is not the Nordics. Sweden's FKAB already designed Trafikverket's new electric ferries. Knud E. Hansen sits in Denmark, Deltamarin in Finland. A purely Norwegian group needs a plan to win in Sweden and Finland, not just at home.
  • Still selling hours. Without standard classes and lifetime work, the result is just a bigger, still cyclical engineering shop.
